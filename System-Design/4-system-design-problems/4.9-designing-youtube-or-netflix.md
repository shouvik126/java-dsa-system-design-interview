# Designing YouTube (or Netflix)

Every case study in this chapter follows the same seven steps from [System Design Interviews: A step by step guide](./4.1-system-design-interviews-a-step-by-step-guide.md), and builds its design from the components in the [System Design Master Template](./4.2-system-design-master-template.md). Keep both open beside you, and try each problem yourself before reading the solution.

---

## Why YouTube?

YouTube stands as the global benchmark for video-sharing and streaming platforms. Millions of users across the globe upload content, stream high-definition videos on demand, interact via likes and comments, and perform instantaneous searches. Subscription-based Video-on-Demand (VOD) services like Netflix share almost the exact same core infrastructure for video chunking, multi-resolution encoding, distributed blob storage, and Content Delivery Network (CDN) edge distribution.

### The Engineering Challenge

From an architectural standpoint, designing YouTube tests your ability to engineer a massive, distributed data platform capable of handling two completely different workloads simultaneously:

1. **Intense Read-Heavy Video Delivery**: Serving petabytes of high-throughput binary video data to millions of concurrent viewers worldwide with minimal startup latency and zero playback buffering.
2. **Asynchronous Heavy-Weight Upload Processing Pipeline**: Ingesting, transcoding, thumbnailing, and indexing hundreds of hours of raw media uploaded every minute without degrading read performance.

With an overwhelming read-to-write traffic ratio of **200:1**, every critical design decision must prioritize optimizing the read path while decoupling the heavy write path.

---

## Step 1: Clarify the Requirements

> [!TIP]
> Always clarify requirements at the beginning of the interview. Ask targeted questions until you define the precise boundary and scale of the system the interviewer expects.

We will design a scalable, production-grade video streaming platform inspired by YouTube and Netflix, focusing on the core functional capabilities and operational requirements listed below.

### Functional Requirements
1. **Video Upload**: Registered users can upload raw video content to the platform.
2. **Video Streaming & Viewing**: Users can seamlessly stream and watch videos on demand across various client devices (mobile, desktop, TV).
3. **Video Search**: Users can search for published videos by title and keywords.
4. **Engagement Metrics**: The system tracks real-time video statistics, including total views, likes, and dislikes.
5. **Comments**: Users can post comments on videos and view existing comment threads.

### Non-Functional Requirements
1. **High Reliability & Durability**: Uploaded media must never be corrupted or permanently lost after ingestion.
2. **High Availability**: The platform prioritizes system availability over absolute immediate data consistency (an **AP** design under the CAP theorem). A slight propagation delay before a newly uploaded video or view-count update appears globally is completely acceptable.
3. **Low Latency Playback**: Video streaming must launch rapidly (sub-second initial playback latency) and provide smooth, uninterrupted playback without buffering.

### Out of Scope
To maintain focus during a standard system design session, the following features are considered out of scope:
- Personalized recommendation algorithms and ML feed ranking.
- Channel subscriptions and user notifications.
- Trending content feeds, watch history, and user playlists/favorites.

> [!NOTE]
> **Understanding the Consistency Trade-off**: Non-functional requirement #2 explicitly trades strict consistency for maximum availability. In Step 6, we will pinpoint exactly where read staleness occurs (e.g., database read-replicas) and why this trade-off is essential for global scale.

## Step 2: Estimate the Scale

System design decisions depend heavily on the magnitude of incoming traffic and storage growth. Let us perform back-of-the-envelope estimations based on realistic production assumptions.

### System Assumptions
- **Total Registered Accounts**: 1.5 Billion
- **Daily Active Users (DAU)**: 800 Million
- **Average Views per DAU**: 5 videos per day

---

### 1. Read Load (Video Views QPS)

$$\text{Total Daily Views} = 800\text{M DAU} \times 5\text{ views/day} = 4\text{ Billion video views per day}$$

$$\text{Read QPS} = \frac{4,000,000,000\text{ views}}{86,400\text{ seconds/day}} \approx 46,300\text{ video views per second}$$

Peak traffic often reaches 2$\times$ the average load, so our system must comfortably handle up to **92,600 peak read QPS**.

---

### 2. Write Load (Video Upload QPS)

Assuming a **1:200 upload-to-view ratio** (for every video uploaded, 200 videos are streamed):

$$\text{Upload QPS} = \frac{46,300\text{ views/sec}}{200} \approx 230\text{ video uploads per second}$$

---

### 3. Storage Capacity Estimations

Assume **500 hours of raw video** are uploaded to the platform every minute. 

Because we store each uploaded video in multiple resolutions (e.g., 1080p, 720p, 480p, 360p) and formats, 1 minute of raw video requires approximately **50 MB** of total storage across all encoded renditions.

$$\text{Storage Growth per Minute} = 500\text{ hours} \times 60\text{ minutes/hour} \times 50\text{ MB} = 1,500\text{ GB per minute}$$

$$\text{Daily Ingestion Rate} = \frac{1,500\text{ GB}}{60\text{ seconds}} = 25\text{ GB per second}$$

$$\text{Total Daily Storage} = 25\text{ GB/sec} \times 86,400\text{ seconds} \approx 2.16\text{ Petabytes (PB) per day}$$

$$\text{5-Year Storage Capacity} = 2.16\text{ PB/day} \times 365\text{ days} \times 5\text{ years} \approx 3.94\text{ Exabytes (EB)}$$

---

### 4. Network Bandwidth Estimations

#### Incoming Bandwidth (Ingress / Uploads)
500 hours of uploaded video per minute equates to 30,000 video-minutes generated per minute. Assuming an uncompressed network transmission size of ~10 MB per minute of uploaded video:

$$\text{Incoming Traffic} = \frac{500\text{ hours} \times 60\text{ mins} \times 10\text{ MB}}{60\text{ seconds}} = 5\text{ GB per second (40 Gbps ingress)}$$

#### Outgoing Bandwidth (Egress / Streaming)
With a 1:200 write-to-read ratio, outgoing video streaming data volume is roughly 200 times higher than incoming ingress:

$$\text{Outgoing Traffic} = 5\text{ GB/sec} \times 200 = 1,000\text{ GB per second} = 1\text{ Terabyte per second (8 Tbps egress)}$$

---

### Summary of Architectural Takeaways from Scale

| Metric | Estimated Value | Direct Impact on Design |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Read:Write Ratio** | **200 : 1** | System is heavily read-dominant. Read paths must be cached aggressively; heavy encoding workloads must be decoupled asynchronously. |
| **Storage Growth** | **25 GB / sec (2.16 PB/day)** | Single-node database storage is impossible. Video binary payloads must be stored in distributed Object Storage (e.g., AWS S3). |
| **Outgoing Bandwidth** | **1 TB / sec** | Origin data centers cannot directly serve outgoing traffic. Content must be pushed to a global Content Delivery Network (CDN). |

## Step 3: Define the API

We can expose the system functionality through clean RESTful APIs over HTTPS. Three primary endpoints define the core interaction: uploading, searching, and streaming.

### 1. Upload Video Endpoint

`POST /v1/videos/upload`

#### Request Parameters
- `api_dev_key` (*string*): Developer authorization key used for authentication and client rate-limiting quotas.
- `title` (*string*): Video title.
- `description` (*string, optional*): Video summary and details.
- `tags` (*string[]*): Array of searchable tags.
- `category_id` (*integer*): Category identifier (e.g., Music, Gaming, Education).
- `default_language` (*string*): Primary audio language code (e.g., `en-US`).
- `recording_location` (*string, optional*): Geographic coordinates or location name.
- `video_payload` (*binary stream*): Binary file payload (uploaded via chunked multipart stream).

#### Response
- **Status**: `HTTP 202 Accepted`
- **Body**:
```json
{
  "status": "ACCEPTED",
  "video_id": "vid_987456321",
  "upload_status_url": "https://api.youtube.com/v1/videos/status?id=vid_987456321",
  "message": "Video upload received successfully. Transcoding queued."
}
```

> [!NOTE]
> **Why HTTP 202?**: Video encoding and thumbnail generation take time. The server accepts the file asynchronously, queues the background job, and immediately returns `HTTP 202 Accepted`. The user can poll `upload_status_url` or receive a push notification when processing completes.

---

### 2. Search Video Endpoint

`GET /v1/videos/search`

#### Request Parameters
- `api_dev_key` (*string*): Developer API key.
- `search_query` (*string*): Search terms entered by the user.
- `user_location` (*string, optional*): Client location for geo-boosted relevance.
- `max_results` (*integer, optional*): Number of results per page (default: 20, max: 50).
- `page_token` (*string, optional*): Pagination token for fetching subsequent result pages.

#### Response
- **Status**: `HTTP 200 OK`
- **Body**:
```json
{
  "next_page_token": "token_page_2_xyz",
  "total_results": 1420,
  "items": [
    {
      "video_id": "vid_987456321",
      "title": "System Design: How to Design YouTube",
      "thumbnail_url": "https://cdn.youtube.com/thumbs/vid_987456321_m.jpg",
      "uploader_id": "usr_445566",
      "views": 154200,
      "created_at": "2026-09-20T10:00:00Z"
    }
  ]
}
```

---

### 3. Stream Video Endpoint

`GET /v1/videos/stream`

#### Request Parameters
- `api_dev_key` (*string*): Developer API key.
- `video_id` (*string*): Unique video resource identifier.
- `offset` (*integer*): Time offset in seconds from which to stream (enables cross-device pause/resume).
- `codec` (*string*): Target media codec supported by client device (e.g., `H.264`, `VP9`, `AV1`).
- `resolution` (*string*): Target display resolution (e.g., `1080p`, `720p`, `480p`).

#### Response
- **Status**: `HTTP 200 OK` or `HTTP 206 Partial Content`
- **Header**: `Content-Type: video/MP2T` or `application/x-mpegURL`
- **Body**: Media stream chunk payload corresponding to the specified offset, codec, and resolution.

## Step 4: Define the Data Model

Our platform manages two fundamentally distinct types of data that require completely different storage systems:

1. **Large Unstructured Media Blobs**: Raw video files, transcoded video chunks, and thumbnail image files. These belong in dedicated distributed **Object Storage** or specialized NoSQL stores.
2. **Structured Application Metadata**: User profile information, video attributes, indexing links, engagement metrics, and comments. These belong in a **Relational SQL Database (MySQL)**.

---

### Database Schema Definition

The entities flow directly from our functional requirements: Users upload Videos, and Users post Comments on Videos.

![Database Schema for YouTube](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shouvik126/HLD_images/main/4.9-youtube-database-schema.svg)

---

### Relational Entity Details (MySQL)

#### `User` Table
Stores account metadata for content creators and viewers.
- `user_id` (*BigInt, Primary Key*): Unique identifier for the user account.
- `name` (*Varchar(100)*): User display name.
- `email` (*Varchar(255), Unique*): User email address.
- `age` (*SmallInt*): User age.
- `created_at` (*Timestamp*): Account registration timestamp.

#### `Video` Table
Stores metadata describing each uploaded video asset.
- `video_id` (*BigInt, Primary Key*): Unique identifier for the video asset.
- `title` (*Varchar(255)*): Video title.
- `description` (*Text*): Detailed video description.
- `size_bytes` (*BigInt*): Size of the original uploaded file in bytes.
- `file_path` (*Varchar(512)*): Object Storage URI pointing to the encoded video manifest file.
- `thumbnail_url` (*Varchar(512)*): Object Storage or Key-Value URI pointing to the thumbnail asset.
- `uploader_user_id` (*BigInt, Foreign Key -> User.user_id*): Identifier of the uploader account.
- `likes_count` (*BigInt*): Total accumulated likes.
- `dislikes_count` (*BigInt*): Total accumulated dislikes.
- `views_count` (*BigInt*): Total accumulated view count.
- `created_at` (*Timestamp*): Upload timestamp.

#### `Comment` Table
Stores user comments associated with specific videos.
- `comment_id` (*BigInt, Primary Key*): Unique comment identifier.
- `video_id` (*BigInt, Foreign Key -> Video.video_id*): Target video identifier.
- `user_id` (*BigInt, Foreign Key -> User.user_id*): Author user identifier.
- `comment_text` (*Text*): Content of the comment.
- `created_at` (*Timestamp*): Timestamp when the comment was created.

---

### Why Use a Relational Database for Metadata?

1. **Structured Relationships**: The metadata exhibits clear entity relationships (e.g., a video belongs to one user; a comment belongs to one video and one user). Relational databases provide strict schema enforcement and efficient primary key lookups.
2. **ACID Transactions for Metadata Updates**: Operations like creating a user account or updating video publishing details require atomic write guarantees.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Handling Scale**: Step 2 estimated 230 video uploads per second, adding ~230 rows/sec to `Video`. However, read QPS on `Video` rows reaches 46,300 QPS. Because a single MySQL server would quickly become overloaded by read traffic and storage limits, in Step 6 we will shard this database across multiple database machines.

## Step 5: Draw the High-Level Design

The high-level architecture separates the **fast synchronous read path** from the **heavy asynchronous write/upload path**.

![High-Level Design of YouTube](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shouvik126/HLD_images/main/4.9-youtube-high-level-design.svg)

---

### Core Architectural Components

1. **API Gateway / Load Balancers**: Entry point that authenticates incoming requests, performs rate-limiting, and routes API traffic to API application servers.
2. **Application Servers**: Handle metadata reads/writes, generate search queries, validate uploads, and return video streaming URLs.
3. **Processing Queue (Message Broker)**: Decouples upload reception from media processing. Uploaded raw video files are placed into a distributed queue (e.g., Apache Kafka or RabbitMQ) for background processing.
4. **Encoding Workers (Transcoders)**: Distributed worker clusters that pull tasks from the queue, transcode raw videos into multiple resolutions and codecs, and generate adaptive bitrate chunks.
5. **Thumbnail Generator**: Worker cluster that extracts representative frames during transcoding and compresses them into small thumbnail images.
6. **Object Storage (Amazon S3 / GCS)**: Distributed blob storage holding raw video uploads, transcoded chunk files, and streaming manifest files.
7. **Metadata Storage (MySQL Database)**: Relational cluster storing user profiles, video metadata, and comments.

## Step 6: Go Deep

Now we deep-dive into the critical sub-systems and engineering trade-offs required to operate YouTube at global scale.

---

### Where are the videos stored?

Video binary files are **write-once, read-many (WORM)** assets. Once transcoded, video chunks are immutable and never updated in place. Two primary storage paradigms fit this access pattern:

```
                  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
                  │          Video Storage Choice           │
                  └────────────────────┬────────────────────┘
                                       │
                ┌──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┐
                ▼                                             ▼
  ┌───────────────────────────┐                 ┌───────────────────────────┐
  │      Object Storage       │                 │  Distributed File System  │
  │     (e.g., Amazon S3)     │                 │       (e.g., HDFS)        │
  └─────────────┬─────────────┘                 └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                │                                             │
  • Native HTTP serving                         • Built for batch analytics
  • Multi-region auto replication               • High setup & ops maintenance
  • Direct CDN integration                      • Optimized for sequential scans
  • RECOMMENDED for Streaming                   • Useful for Big Data jobs
```

#### 1. Object Storage (Recommended: Amazon S3 / Google Cloud Storage)
- **Why it fits**: Object stores organize data as flat blobs identified by keys and serve them directly over HTTP. They natively provide $99.999999999\%$ data durability through automatic multi-region replication.
- **CDN Integration**: Object stores seamlessly interface with CDNs for edge caching.
- **Operational Cost**: Managed object storage eliminates manual server rack management, disk failure replacements, and capacity rebalancing.

#### 2. Distributed File System (Alternative: HDFS / GlusterFS)
- **Why it is less ideal**: Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS) is designed for large sequential batch analytical reads across big data files. It requires operating dedicated NameNodes, managing block replication manually, and handling HTTP gateway wrappers.
- **Verdict**: Use **Object Storage** for video asset delivery.

---

### How should we manage read traffic?

With a **200:1 read-to-write ratio**, read queries must never contention-lock database write operations.

```
 Client Write                  Client Read
      │                             │
      ▼                             ▼
┌───────────┐                ┌─────────────┐
│  Primary  │─ Replication ─►│  Secondary  │
│ MySQL DB  │    Lag (ms)    │ Replica DBs │
└───────────┘                └─────────────┘
 (Handles                     (Handles Read
   Writes)                     Queries)
```

1. **Read/Write Decoupling**: Separate database traffic cleanly. All database mutations (new uploads, comment additions) hit the **Primary MySQL** instance.
2. **Primary-Secondary (Master-Replica) Replication**: Asynchronously replicate data from the Primary DB to multiple **Secondary Read Replicas**. Read replicas serve all query traffic for video metadata and search details.
3. **Managing Eventual Consistency**: Replicating data to secondaries introduces a tiny replication lag (typically a few milliseconds). A viewer might occasionally query a read-replica before a brand-new video metadata row has propagated, resulting in temporary missing results. As specified in Step 1's non-functional requirements, we explicitly accept this short staleness in exchange for infinite horizontal read scalability.

---

### Where are thumbnails stored?

Thumbnails present a completely different storage challenge than video files:

- **Volume & Access Pattern**: A single video requires 5 different thumbnail renditions. Storing 1 billion videos creates **5 billion thumbnail files**.
- **Small Payload, Astronomical Read QPS**: While a video file is large (~100 MB), a thumbnail is tiny (~5 KB). However, when a viewer browses YouTube's homepage, a single page request fetches 30 to 50 thumbnail images simultaneously!

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        Thumbnail Storage Issue                         │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Plain File System: Millions of 5KB files = Excessive disk head seeks    │
│ Solution: Bigtable / Key-Value Store (Aggregates files into SSTables)  │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

#### Why Standard File Systems Fail
Storing billions of 5 KB files on standard Linux file systems (like ext4) causes massive performance degradation due to **random disk head seeks** and directory inode table bottlenecks. Reading hundreds of separate tiny files requires hundreds of physical disk head movements.

#### The Solution: Bigtable / Wide-Column Key-Value Storage
- **Bigtable / Cassandra**: Stores multiple small thumbnail binary blobs combined inside consolidated, sequential SSTable file blocks on disk. This enables lightning-fast sequential disk reads and efficient index lookups.
- **In-Memory Caching**: Put an in-memory caching tier (**Redis / Memcached**) in front of Bigtable. Because thumbnails are small, a cluster of cache servers can keep the top 20% most popular thumbnails entirely in RAM.

### Video uploads

Uploading massive video files (often several gigabytes in size) over unpredictable mobile networks requires fault-tolerant handling.

```
 Client                                                Server
   │                                                     │
   │─── 1. Initiate Upload Request ─────────────────────►│ (Returns Upload Session ID)
   │                                                     │
   │─── 2. Send Chunk 0 (0MB - 10MB) ───────────────────►│ (Acknowledged)
   │─── 3. Send Chunk 1 (10MB - 20MB) ──────────────────►│ (Acknowledged)
   │     ❌ Network Drops Interrupts Upload!              │
   │                                                     │
   │─── 4. Query Resume Offset (Session ID) ────────────►│ (Server replies: Chunk 1 received)
   │─── 5. Resume Send Chunk 2 (20MB - 30MB) ───────────►│ (Upload completes smoothly!)
```

1. **Resumable Chunked Multipart Uploads**: Clients break a raw video file into small, uniform chunks (e.g., 5 MB to 10 MB per chunk) using standard protocols like **TUS** or AWS S3 Multipart Upload.
2. **Pause and Resume Capability**: If a user's network connection drops at 80% completion, the client queries the server for the last verified byte offset. The client resumes uploading strictly from the missing chunk, preventing users from ever having to restart a multi-gigabyte upload from the beginning.

### Video encoding

When a raw video upload finishes, it cannot be streamed directly to viewers. Raw video recorded on an iPhone or DSLR camera uses formats (e.g., Apple ProRes or raw AVI) and resolutions unsuited for low-bandwidth mobile devices.

### The Transcoding Pipeline

```
┌──────────────────┐    ┌──────────────────┐    ┌──────────────────┐    ┌──────────────────┐
│  Raw Video File  │───►│  Segment Worker  │───►│ Transcode Matrix │───►│  Manifest File   │
│  (Uploaded Blob) │    │  (Splits Chunks) │    │ (Codecs & Specs) │    │  (.m3u8 / .mpd)  │
└──────────────────┘    └──────────────────┘    └──────────────────┘    └──────────────────┘
```

1. **Asynchronous Dequeuing**: Uploading a video creates a task in the **Processing Queue**. Transcoding worker instances pull tasks from the queue asynchronously.
2. **Chunk Segmentation**: The input video is divided into 2-to-10 second video segment chunks.
3. **Multi-Format Transcoding**: Each segment chunk is encoded into multiple target formats and codecs:
   - **Codecs**: `H.264` (universal device compatibility), `VP9` (higher compressionEfficiency for web), and `AV1` (state-of-the-art compression for ultra-low bandwidth).
   - **Resolutions**: `4K (2160p)`, `1080p`, `720p`, `480p`, `360p`, `240p`.
4. **Adaptive Bitrate Streaming (HLS / MPEG-DASH)**:
   - The encoder creates streaming protocol manifest files: **HLS** (`.m3u8` playlist) or **MPEG-DASH** (`.mpd` manifest).
   - During playback, the client's video player dynamically monitors real-time network download speeds. If WiFi drops to cellular 3G, the player automatically switches to fetching 360p chunks without stopping playback!

### Metadata sharding

As the `Video` table grows to billions of rows, a single MySQL instance runs out of storage and CPU memory. We must **shard** the database across multiple physical database instances.

```
                               ┌───────────────────────────┐
                               │ Metadata Sharding Strategy│
                               └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                                             │
               ┌─────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┐
               ▼                                                           ▼
┌─────────────────────────────┐                             ┌─────────────────────────────┐
│    Sharding by UserID       │                             │    Sharding by VideoID      │
├─────────────────────────────┤                             ├─────────────────────────────┤
│ Hash(UserID) -> Shard ID    │                             │ Hash(VideoID) -> Shard ID   │
│ • Fast lookup of user files │                             │ • Uniform data distribution │
│ ❌ Hotspot on popular users │                             │ ❌ User query needs scatter │
│ ❌ Search requires scatter  │                             │   gather across all shards  │
└─────────────────────────────┘                             └─────────────────────────────┘
```

#### Strategy A: Sharding based on `UserID`
- **Mechanism**: `Shard_ID = Hash(UserID) % Total_Shards`. All metadata for videos uploaded by a given user sits on one database node.
- **Drawbacks**:
  1. **Hotspot Bottleneck (Celebrity Problem)**: A famous content creator (e.g., PewDiePie or MrBeast) uploads a video. Millions of users query that video simultaneously. The single database node holding that creator's `UserID` receives all read queries and collapses.
  2. **Uneven Storage Distribution**: A user uploading 10,000 videos consumes vast storage on one node, while casual users consume virtually nothing.

#### Strategy B: Sharding based on `VideoID` (Recommended)
- **Mechanism**: `Shard_ID = Hash(VideoID) % Total_Shards`. Each video's metadata is distributed randomly across the database cluster.
- **Advantages**:
  1. **Uniform Data Distribution**: Prevents storage imbalances.
  2. **Eliminates Uploader Hotspots**: Popular videos are distributed evenly across different shards.
- **Handling Popular Video Reads**: The hotspot problem shifts from *creator accounts* to *popular individual videos*. We solve this by placing a caching layer in front of the database shards to absorb 99% of hot `VideoID` queries.

---

### Video deduplication

Users frequently upload duplicate copies of popular videos, movie trailers, or copyrighted content. Duplicate videos waste petabytes of object storage, transcoding compute cycles, cache memory, and network bandwidth.

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        Inline Video Deduplication                      │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Client Upload Stream ──► Perceptual Hashing / Feature Extraction       │
│                                    │                                   │
│                        Is Hash Match Found in Index?                   │
│                       /                             \                  │
│                     YES                              NO                │
│                      │                                │                │
│    Stop Upload & Reuse Object Pointer      Complete Full Transcode     │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

1. **Inline Deduplication**: Perform deduplication **during** video ingestion rather than as a post-processing job. As the upload begins, extract perceptual video fingerprints (using algorithms like **Block Matching** or **Phase Correlation**).
2. **Fingerprint Index Lookup**: Compare the incoming fingerprint against a global fingerprint index.
3. **Deduplication Logic**:
   - **Exact Duplicate**: Immediately stop the upload. Point the new `VideoID` metadata row to the existing encoded object storage blob.
   - **Partial / Clip Duplicate**: If the video is a short clip extracted from a longer existing video, segment the file and store only the missing unique chunks.

### Load balancing

Video distribution requires intelligent traffic routing across cache nodes and data centers.

```
                        ┌───────────────────────────────┐
                        │   Consistent Hashing Ring     │
                        └───────────────┬───────────────┘
                                        │
           ┌────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────┐
           ▼                            ▼                            ▼
  ┌─────────────────┐          ┌─────────────────┐          ┌─────────────────┐
  │ Cache Server A  │          │ Cache Server B  │          │ Cache Server C  │
  └────────┬────────┘          └─────────────────┘          └─────────────────┘
           │ (Overloaded by Viral Video!)
           ▼
  Client Redirected via HTTP 302 / 307
           │
           ▼
  ┌─────────────────┐
  │ Cache Server D  │  (Serves content smoothly)
  └─────────────────┘
```

1. **Consistent Hashing**: Use consistent hashing to map video keys across cache nodes. This ensures smooth load distribution and minimizes cache re-mapping key movement when cache servers fail or scale up.
2. **Handling Viral Content ("Hot Spots")**: If a breaking news video goes viral, consistent hashing maps that `VideoID` to a single cache hostname. That single physical server would become overwhelmed by millions of concurrent requests.
3. **Dynamic HTTP Redirection**: When a cache server detects its network interface or CPU utilization exceeding 80%, it responds to new incoming requests with an `HTTP 302 Found` or `HTTP 307 Temporary Redirect` header, pointing the client to an underutilized mirror cache node within the same POP.

### Cache

Caching is critical to maintaining low playback startup latency and protecting origin databases.

```
  Application Server Request
              │
              ▼
    ┌───────────────────┐        Cache Hit (80% of Traffic)
    │  Redis / Memcached│──────────────────────────────────► Return Row
    └─────────┬─────────┘
              │ Cache Miss (20% of Traffic)
              ▼
    ┌───────────────────┐
    │ MySQL DB Shard    │
    └───────────────────┘
```

#### Application Cache Tier
Position an in-memory caching tier (**Redis** or **Memcached**) between Application Servers and Database Shards to store hot `Video` and `User` metadata rows.

#### Eviction Policy: LRU (Least Recently Used)
Adopt an **LRU (Least Recently Used)** eviction policy. Video popularity decays rapidly over time. As a video ages and view volume declines, its metadata naturally drops out of memory to make space for newly trending content.

#### Caching Strategy: The 80-20 Rule (Pareto Principle)
In video streaming, **20% of popular videos generate 80% of total read traffic**. 

Rather than trying to cache all 1 billion videos, we only need to cache the top **20% of daily read volume**. 

$$\text{Daily Read Volume} = 4\text{ Billion views/day} \times 100\text{ MB/video} = 400\text{ Petabytes/day}$$

$$\text{20% Target Cache Size} = 0.20 \times 400\text{ PB} = 80\text{ Petabytes globally across CDN & Edge Caches}$$

### Content Delivery Network (CDN)

A Content Delivery Network (CDN) is a geographically distributed network of edge proxy servers designed to deliver media content to viewers with ultra-low latency.

```
 Viewer (Tokyo) ──────► CDN Edge Server (Tokyo POP) [CACHE HIT] ──► Instant Streaming
                                 │
                                 │ [CACHE MISS for Cold Video]
                                 ▼
                      Origin Object Storage (US-East)
```

1. **Edge POP Delivery**: When a user in Tokyo requests a video, the request is routed via DNS/Anycast to the nearest Tokyo CDN edge Point of Presence (POP). If the video chunk exists in the edge cache, it streams directly over local network hops, eliminating trans-oceanic latency.
2. **Tiered Caching Strategy**:
   - **Popular / Viral Content (Hot)**: Cached aggressively at edge CDN POPs close to end users.
   - **Obscure Content (Cold - < 20 views/day)**: Not stored on CDNs to avoid paying expensive edge storage eviction fees. Requests for cold videos pass through the CDN and fetch chunks directly from origin **Object Storage** data centers.
3. **The Immutable File Advantage**: Because encoded video files are **immutable** (never modified), CDN edge servers never have to deal with complex cache invalidation or cache staleness protocols. Video chunk URLs can be cached indefinitely with long `Cache-Control: max-age` HTTP headers!

## Step 7: Bottlenecks and Failure Points

A robust system design must account for unexpected component outages and traffic spikes.

```
┌───────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Failure Point             │ Architectural Mitigation Strategy                                       │
├───────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Database Node Failure  │ • Primary-Secondary automated failover via Orchestrator / Raft consensus.│
│                           │ • Consistent hashing minimizes data movement during database re-sharding.│
├───────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. Viral Video Surge      │ • Multi-tiered CDN edge caching absorbs 99% of bandwidth load.          │
│                           │ • Dynamic HTTP 302 redirections divert excess traffic to mirror nodes.  │
├───────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. Processing Queue       │ • Complete architectural isolation between write pipeline & read path.  │
│    Backlog / Failure      │ • Video viewing depends strictly on Object Storage & CDNs. Queue backlog │
│                           │   delays new uploads but NEVER interrupts existing video playback!      │
├───────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 4. Stale Metadata Reads   │ • Explicitly accepted AP trade-off. Read-replica lag lasts milliseconds,│
│                           │   which is imperceptible to users in real-world scenarios.              │
└───────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Key Strength - Read/Write Isolation**: Emphasize during your interview that the read path (watching videos via CDNs and caches) is completely decoupled from the write processing pipeline (encoding queue and workers). Even if the entire encoding pipeline crashes, millions of viewers can continue streaming videos without interruption.

## Where AI Fits in This Design

In modern production platforms, Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning models integrate seamlessly into the **asynchronous background processing pipeline**.

```
                           ┌───────────────────────────┐
                           │ Upload Processing Queue   │
                           └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                                         │
                 ┌───────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
                 ▼                                               ▼
   ┌───────────────────────────┐                   ┌───────────────────────────┐
   │  Speech-to-Text Model     │                   │  Safety & Moderation      │
   │   (Automated Captions)    │                   │   (Content Classifier)    │
   └─────────────┬─────────────┘                   └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                 │                                               │
   • Generates VTT transcript                       • Scans video frames & audio
   • Non-blocking processing                        • Flags rule violations
   • Saved alongside Metadata                      • Holds publishing if flagged
```

### 1. Automated Closed Captioning (Speech-to-Text)
- **Model**: Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) models (e.g., OpenAI Whisper or custom deep learning models).
- **Pipeline Integration**: As an encoding worker outputs video chunks, an ASR task is dispatched to transcribe audio into text timed captions (`.vtt` or `.srt` files).
- **Execution Policy**: **Non-Blocking**. If the caption generation service is slow or fails, the video is published anyway. Captions can be appended to video metadata asynchronously whenever processing completes.

### 2. Automated Content Moderation & Copyright Protection
- **Model**: Computer vision classifiers and audio fingerprinting models (e.g., YouTube Content ID).
- **Pipeline Integration**: Scans uploaded video frames and audio tracks for visual violence, adult content, or copyrighted audio matches.
- **Execution Policy**: **Product-Dependent Blocking**. Unlike captioning, moderation may act as a publishing gatekeeper. If the model flags severe rule violations with high confidence, the video state is set to `Pending Human Review` before it is made visible to the public.

### Cost & Infrastructure Realities
AI inference on video streams requires specialized **GPU clusters** (e.g., NVIDIA H100/T4 instances). Processing models are billed per minute of processed video. Therefore, AI processing tasks must be carefully scoped within the background queue to avoid unnecessary compute overhead on rejected duplicate uploads.

## Summary Takeaways

| Design Dimension | Solution Chosen | Key Technical Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **System Workload** | Read-Heavy (200:1 ratio) | Optimize read path with CDNs and LRU caches; decouple write processing with queues. |
| **Video Blob Storage** | Object Storage (Amazon S3) | High durability ($99.999999999\%$), native HTTP streaming, multi-region replication. |
| **Thumbnail Storage** | Bigtable / Wide-Column Store | Avoids small file inode seek bottlenecks by packing 5KB images sequentially on disk. |
| **Metadata Storage** | Sharded MySQL (`VideoID`) | Relational schema integrity for entities; sharded by `VideoID` to avoid uploader hotspots. |
| **Upload Strategy** | Resumable Chunked Multipart | TUS protocol prevents network drops from restarting multi-gigabyte uploads. |
| **Encoding Pipeline** | Asynchronous Queue & Workers | Decouples encoding from client upload HTTP response (`HTTP 202 Accepted`). |
| **Playback Delivery** | Multi-Tier CDN Edge POPs | Delivers sub-second playback latency globally; immutable video chunks eliminate cache staleness. |
