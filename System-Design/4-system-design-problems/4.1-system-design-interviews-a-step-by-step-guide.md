# System Design Interviews: A Step-by-Step Guide

Most engineers find system design interviews more challenging than coding interviews, and the cause is rarely a lack of technical knowledge. The real difficulty is that system design questions have no fixed structure. You are handed a single sentence, a blank whiteboard, roughly forty-five minutes, and no explicit blueprint for what a complete answer looks like.

A structured process solves this. Having a systematic sequence to follow ensures you always know what step comes next, and allows you to confirm at the end whether you covered all necessary architectural ground.

Below is the seven-step framework. Every case study follows this process, and so should you.

![Seven Steps Time Breakdown](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shouvik126/HLD_images/main/4.1-seven-steps-time-breakdown.png)

> [!NOTE]
> The minute allocations are a guide rather than a rigid rule. What matters is the shape of the interview: a brief opening, a quick middle phase, and the vast majority of your time spent deep-diving into two or three core challenges. Candidates who fail almost always get this backward by spending half the session on the initial two steps.

---

## Step 1: Clarify the Requirements

Never start designing until you are certain what you are building. Spend roughly five minutes agreeing on what features the system must provide and the scale it must support.

1. **Functional Requirements**: Pick three to five core features. For a Twitter-like service, these might be posting tweets, following users, and viewing the home timeline. Then explicitly state what you are leaving out (e.g., search, direct messaging, trending topics). Stating exclusions out loud allows the interviewer to redirect you in minute two rather than minute thirty if you omit something critical.
2. **Non-Functional Requirements**: Establish quantitative metrics: total user base, daily active traffic, target read latency, and acceptable data staleness. If the interviewer declines to provide specific numbers, state a reasonable assumption and move forward.

![Scoping Inclusions vs Exclusions](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shouvik126/HLD_images/main/4.1-scoping-exclusions.png)

> **Key Scoping Insight**: Scoping is as much about what you exclude as what you include. Explicitly vocalizing your exclusions earns significant marks. For a deep dive into scoping, see [Functional vs. Non-Functional Requirements](../1-introduction-to-system-design-interview/1.2-functional-vs-non-functional-requirements.md).

---

## Step 2: Estimate the Scale

Convert the agreed metrics into figures you can design against. Spend three to four minutes calculating out loud, rounding figures aggressively.

Focus on four key metrics:
- **Peak Requests Per Second (QPS)**
- **Storage Capacity** over a 1 to 5-year horizon
- **Inbound and Outbound Bandwidth**
- **Read-to-Write Ratio**

The **read-to-write ratio** is the most vital metric because it dictates downstream architectural choices. A 100:1 read-to-write ratio points directly toward aggressive caching layers and database read replicas. Conversely, a write-heavy ratio points toward message queues and write-optimized storage engines.

![Scale Figures and Read Write Ratio](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shouvik126/HLD_images/main/4.1-scale-figures.png)

Precision is not the goal here. The estimations should visibly drive your subsequent choices—so when adding a cache in step five, you can reference the exact read volume that justified it. For full estimation methodology, see [Back-of-the-Envelope Estimations](../1-introduction-to-system-design-interview/1.3-back-of-the-envelope-estimations.md).

---

## Step 3: Define the API

Write out the primary API endpoints implied by your feature set. Spending three or four minutes on API signatures serves two crucial purposes:

1. It forces vague requirements to become concrete engineering contracts. If you cannot write the signature, you haven't pinned down the feature.
2. It surface misunderstandings early: an interviewer who disagrees with your feature scope will speak up the moment they see your parameters.

![API Parameter Definitions](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shouvik126/HLD_images/main/4.1-api-parameters.png)

Limit this to three or four key calls. For example, in a Twitter-like service:

```http
postTweet(user_id, content, media_ids, timestamp)
getTimeline(user_id, cursor, limit)
followUser(follower_id, followee_id)
```

> **Note on Pagination**: Notice the `cursor` parameter in `getTimeline`. Timelines must be paginated by cursor rather than page offset because the feed updates continuously under the reader as new tweets arrive.

---

## Step 4: Define the Data Model

Identify core entities, essential fields, and their relationships, then specify the appropriate datastore for each.

For a Twitter-like service:

| Entity | Key Fields |
| :--- | :--- |
| **User** | `userId`, `name`, `email`, `createdAt` |
| **Tweet** | `tweetId`, `userId`, `content`, `createdAt` |
| **Follow** | `followerId`, `followeeId` |
| **Media** | `mediaId`, `tweetId`, `storagePath` |

The most valuable part of this step is stating the hot queries and matching them to the optimal database:

> *"The core hot queries are: (1) fetch a tweet by ID, and (2) fetch recent tweets from followed users. Neither requires complex joins, both are key lookups over billions of rows, so a wide-column NoSQL store fits better than a relational SQL database."*

![Entities and Hot Queries](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shouvik126/HLD_images/main/4.1-entities-hot-queries.png)

Large media files belong in dedicated object storage (e.g., AWS S3), storing only the media URI or storage path in the database table.

---

## Step 5: Draw the High-Level Design

Now draw your architecture diagram. Add five or six primary components starting from the client inward, explaining the rationale for each component as you add it.

Build the diagram incrementally rather than presenting a pre-drawn block of 15 components. An instant multi-box diagram looks like a memorized template, whereas an incrementally built diagram demonstrates active architectural reasoning.

![High-Level Design Boxes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shouvik126/HLD_images/main/4.1-high-level-design.png)

Spend about ten minutes here. By the end, you should have an end-to-end data flow: client request entering, being processed by application logic and storage, and returning a response. Avoid diving into granular details prematurely; agree on the overall shape with the interviewer first.

---

## Step 6: Go Deep on Two or Three Things

This is the longest step and carries the heaviest evaluation weight. Identify the subsystems that present genuine technical complexity for this specific problem and dig deep.

- **Twitter-like Service**: Timeline generation strategies and the celebrity fan-out problem.
- **URL Shortener**: Unique key generation algorithms and cache sizing.
- **Real-Time Chat**: WebSockets connection management and message ordering.

Let the interviewer guide which component to explore. When presenting a solution, always discuss the alternative approach and justify your choice:

> *"I could generate follower timelines at write time (fan-out on write) or construct them at read time (fan-out on read). Write-time generation makes reads cheap, which suits a 100:1 read ratio. However, a celebrity with 50 million followers turns a single tweet into 50 million writes. Therefore, I would fan-out on write for standard accounts and merge celebrity tweets at read time."*

![Hard Subsystems Deep Dive](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shouvik126/HLD_images/main/4.1-deep-dive.png)

## Step 7: Identify Bottlenecks and Failure Points

Conclude by identifying where the system reaches its limits and how it handles component failures. A complete answer addresses both:

### 1. Where Does the System Saturate First?
Every design has one bottleneck component that reaches capacity before the others. Name it, state the threshold metric that causes it, and present a mitigation strategy.
*Example: "At roughly 50,000 writes per second, a single primary database becomes the bottleneck; at that threshold, I would shard the database by `userId`."*

### 2. What Happens When a Component Fails?
Identify Single Points of Failure (SPOFs), replication levels, and degraded operational modes. What happens if a database node dies, a queue backs up, or a downstream service experiences latency spikes?

Finally, specify two or three core metrics to monitor for this system (e.g., p99 read latency, fanout queue depth, and cache hit ratio).

![Bottlenecks and Failures](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shouvik126/HLD_images/main/4.1-bottlenecks-failures.png)

Volunteering bottleneck and failure analysis before being prompted is a strong indicator of senior engineering maturity.

---

## The Steps Are a Spine, Not a Rigid Script

Keep two principles in mind to maintain flexibility:

1. **Follow Interviewer Interruptions**: If the interviewer shifts focus (e.g., jumping to the data model while discussing the API), follow their lead smoothly. The 7 steps exist as a checklist for ground to cover, not a rigid script.
2. **Adapt Order When Needed**: Some problems require adjusting the sequence. For example, a typeahead search service is hard to estimate before sketching the high-level design. State your intent out loud: *"I will return to scale estimations once our high-level architecture is established."*

> 💡 **Interview Tip**: State your plan out loud in the first 30 seconds:  
> *"I'll spend 5 minutes clarifying requirements and scale estimations, sketch the high-level design, and then deep-dive into whichever components you prefer."*  
> Stating this framework demonstrates experience and gives you the autonomy to guide the requirements phase smoothly.

---

## Key Takeaway

The seven steps are:
1. **Clarify Requirements**
2. **Estimate Scale**
3. **Define API**
4. **Define Data Model**
5. **Draw High-Level Design**
6. **Go Deep on 2–3 Components**
7. **Identify Bottlenecks & Failure Points**

Keep the opening concise, spend the bulk of your time deep in the most complex subsystems, and conclude by analyzing scale ceilings and failure resilience.
